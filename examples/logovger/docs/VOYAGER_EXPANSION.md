# Voyager: where we diverge from Cepheus, on purpose

Cepheus is absorbed faithfully. Every rule in the graph cites the
book, and where the book is wrong we mark it and report upstream
rather than quietly correcting it.

This file is the other thing: changes Voyager makes BECAUSE it is a
different setting, not because the source is defective. They belong in
their own layer, so the Cepheus layer stays a true reading of the book
and a reader can always see which rule came from where.

Nothing here is decided. These are notes with their evidence attached,
written when the idea arrived so the reasoning is not lost.

---

## Aging is calibrated for 1977, and the book knows it

_Owner, 2026-08-11, at the point of ingesting the aging table._

**The observation.** Physical decline starting at 34 is an assumption
from when this rules family was written, not a fact about a setting
with jump drives. Life expectancy has moved a long way since the
eighties alone. The table is absorbed faithfully because that is the
job, but the calibration is expected to be wrong for Voyager and will
be tuned.

**What the absorbed table actually does.** Aging is 2D6 minus total
terms served, and any total of 0 or less takes characteristics away
permanently:

| terms | age | chance of permanent loss that term |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 34 | 16.7% |
| 5 | 38 | 27.8% |
| 6 | 42 | 41.7% |
| 7 | 46 | **58.3%** |

Reaching the seven-term cap with **no** aging loss at all happens
14.6% of the time. So a 46-year-old is more likely than not to lose
something permanent in a given four years, and a career soldier who
serves out is very probably diminished by the end. That reads as a
statement about 1977 rather than about a spacefaring century.

**The dial is already in the book.** Cepheus has anagathics: "While
using anagathic drugs, the character effectively does not age - add
the number of terms since the character started taking anagathics as a
positive Dice Modifier to rolls on the aging table." That modifier
cancels the term count exactly, which means the rules already contain
a clean, cited way to move the curve without inventing a mechanism.

So the Voyager question is not "how do we rewrite aging". It is **how
available anagathics are**, which is a setting decision: who can get
them, what they cost, what stopping does to you, and whether the
frontier sees them at all. The lore already leans that way, with the
core comfortable and watchful and the Reach scarce in everything.

**Open:** whether Voyager tunes the curve directly, or leaves the
table untouched and expresses the change entirely through anagathic
availability. The second keeps the Cepheus layer a true reading of the
book and puts the divergence where it belongs, in the setting.

## Six independent rolls make some people good at everything

_Owner, 2026-08-11, watching lives generate._

**The observation.** Characteristics are six independent 2D6 rolls, so
some characters come out strong across the board and others weak
across the board. That is not what people are like. Someone strong in
most things usually pays for it somewhere, and someone weak in most
things usually has something else going for them: a knack, a piece of
luck, a thing nobody saw coming.

**What the numbers actually say**, computed from the Characteristic
Modifier table as absorbed:

| total DM across all six | share of characters |
|---|---|
| -3 or worse | 11.4% |
| -2 | 12.1% |
| 0 | 19.0% |
| +2 | 12.1% |
| +3 or better | 11.4% |

So **about one character in nine is poor at nearly everything**, with
nothing in the rules to compensate, and one in nine is good at nearly
everything, with nothing to cost them. The mean DM per characteristic
is exactly zero, which is the point: the system is fair on average and
lumpy in individuals, and it is the individual you have to play.

**The direction.** A compensating trait: a hidden skill, a run of
luck, an inheritance, an enemy who turns out useful. Something that is
not visible on the sheet at creation and is found out later.

**What already points this way.** The setting draft says psionic
strength "is not rolled at creation. It is found out, usually badly,
usually later", and Cepheus itself gates Psi behind the Referee's
permission. That is exactly the shape being described here: a hidden
trait, discovered in play, that changes what a person is worth. The
compensating trait may not need to be a new mechanism so much as a
generalisation of one the setting already has.

**Open, and worth deciding together rather than alone:**

1. Is the trait universal, and merely more visible in a character the
   dice were unkind to, or does it exist only for them? Universal is
   less special-case; conditional is more dramatic and risks reading
   as a consolation prize.
2. Rolled, chosen, or discovered? Rolled keeps it honest and citable.
   Chosen makes it a character decision. Discovered in play is the
   most Voyager, and the hardest to build.
3. Does the referee rule on it, as with psionics and mishaps? There is
   already a `JudgmentPoint` concept for exactly this.
4. Does a strong character pay anything? The observation cuts both
   ways, and only compensating the weak makes the strong strictly
   better off.

**Where it would live.** A Voyager seed layer over the Cepheus one, so
the Cepheus rules keep citing the book unchanged. Rank, aging and
mustering-out all already read their data from rule-owned tables, so a
layer can add a step without touching what is there.
